Log 003 · 2026-11-24

Start small, stay under 55 lb

The aircraft we want in the end carries 20 kg. The aircraft we are building first carries 1.5 kg. This entry explains the gap.

The plan

  • Simulation first. Before buying a single part, we fly the whole delivery mission in software: vertical takeoff, transition to the wing, a route, a hover and release, the return leg, and landing. Then we repeat it in wind and with failures injected.
  • A 2.2 m prototype. About 7.5 kg at takeoff, four lift motors, one pusher. Its job is to teach us the transition and the cargo bay door. We expect to break it.
  • The 55. Just under 55 lb, about 5 kg of cargo. This is the first aircraft that can do useful work on a real route.
  • Heavy. The 20 kg aircraft, built once the smaller one has earned it.

Why this order

Fifty-five pounds is where the FAA's small-aircraft rules end. Below it, a certified remote pilot can test fly a new design. Above it, each flight needs case-by-case permission. Every lesson the big aircraft needs can be learned below the line, at a fraction of the cost per mistake.

Log 002 · 2026-11-23

The range math: what a battery can and cannot do

Our starting wish list was 20 kg of cargo, 200 to 300 km out, and the flight home, all on batteries. The arithmetic says those four things do not fit in one aircraft today.

The estimate

  • A 20 kg payload leads to an aircraft of roughly 75 kg at takeoff.
  • Carrying 25 kg or more of battery, it covers about 120 to 180 km in total. That is a 60 to 90 km radius if it has to come back.
  • Four vertical phases per round trip (two takeoffs, two landings) use roughly a fifth of the battery before the aircraft goes anywhere.

Scaling the aircraft down does not help much. A 24.9 kg electric version with 5 kg of cargo lands at about 130 km in total. The limit is how much energy a kilogram of battery holds, and size does not change that.

What we decided

Gasoline holds more than ten times the usable energy per kilogram. A small engine driving the pusher propeller, with batteries only for the minutes of vertical flight, reaches 600 km with about 10 to 12 kg of fuel. So the long-range aircraft will be a hybrid, and the short-range aircraft will be electric. We would rather publish that trade than promise a range the physics does not support.

Log 001 · 2026-11-23

Why we dropped the folding wings

The first sketch was a cylinder that stood on its tail: a ring of blades around the body, wings and fins that popped out in flight, and legs that retracted after takeoff. It looked great. We kept one part of it.

What stayed

  • The round fuselage. A cylinder is the cheapest body to build. It can be cut from tube stock with no molds, and the cargo bay sits in the belly under the wing, so the balance does not change between a full bay and an empty one.
  • Redundant lift motors. The working aircraft uses eight in four coaxial pairs, so it can land after losing one.

What went

  • Standing on its tail. A tall aircraft with a high center of gravity tips in a crosswind, the transition to forward flight is the hardest kind to control, and the cargo bay rotates ninety degrees along with everything else.
  • Folding wings, pop-out fins, retracting legs. Each one adds weight, cost and a way to fail. A wing that detaches on the ground for transport does the same job.
  • The blade ring. It needs a large bearing and a custom drive that nobody sells.

The result

A lift-plus-cruise layout: fixed wing, two tail booms carrying the lift motors, one pusher propeller, an inverted-V tail and fixed skids. The only moving parts are propellers, control surfaces and the cargo door.

The cost: stopped lift propellers add drag in cruise, which we estimate takes 10 to 15 percent off the range. We think that is a fair price for an aircraft with almost nothing to break.
